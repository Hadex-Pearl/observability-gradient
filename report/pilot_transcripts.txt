**Executive Summary: Micromobility Adoption Study (Q3 Report)**

**Fieldwork and Evidence Base**
Fieldwork is complete, comprising three survey waves and thirty interviews. This synthesis integrates the findings and their implications for the upcoming permit renewal decision.

**Headline Findings**

1. **Usage is higher than operator-reported figures.** Self-reported usage stands at 31.4% (95% CI 28.8–34.0; n=1,208). The gap between survey and operator data likely reflects either recall inflation or household account-sharing; current evidence cannot distinguish between these explanations.

2. **The substitution pattern weakens the environmental case but strengthens the access case.** When asked what mode they would otherwise have used, respondents cited walking (34.1%), bus (26.8%), and train/tram (14.2%), with only 11.3% naming private car. A further 8.9% would not have made the trip at all. This pattern is stable across all waves and demographic groups. Notably, car substitution cannot be predicted by any identifiable cohort (pseudo R² = 0.08), suggesting it is diffuse rather than concentrated.

3. **Infrastructure, not vehicles, is the primary barrier to use.** Lapsed users cited route safety and lack of protected lanes more often than cost, availability, or vehicle quality. Survey data corroborate this: safety ranks second only to cost among non-users’ reasons for non-use. One ring-road stretch was named unprompted by multiple interviewees.

**Decision-Relevant Implications**
The evidence supports **renewal on revised terms**, not renewal on current terms. Three specific conditions should be attached to any renewed permit:

- **Protected-lane provision** on the routes carrying the highest trip volumes;
- A **parking bay requirement** modelled on the Lyon and Gothenburg schemes;
- A **reporting condition** requiring operator-level trip data to resolve the current discrepancy with self-reported usage.

**Caveats and Limitations**
Three limitations temper confidence in the findings. First, waves one and two fell in February and May, confounding seasonality with time trends—although a wave-two question on typical annual usage partly mitigates this. Second, four of six operators refused to share trip data, so all usage figures rest on self-report. Third, a wave-one instrument problem required postcode recoding for approximately 9% of responses (documented in methods).

**Emergent Theme Warranting Further Attention**
A theme outside the original brief emerged in interviews: several participants described shifting from timetable-based journey planning to spontaneous departure. If generalisable, this represents a value—and a service-reliability requirement—not captured by trip-count metrics. It may warrant incorporation into future service specifications.

**Methods**

**Design**

The study used a mixed-methods design running from January to September, combining a repeated cross-sectional survey of city residents with semi-structured interviews with a purposive subsample of survey respondents. The two components were intended to be complementary: the survey provided population-level patterns of usage and demographic correlates, while the interviews offered depth on decision-making processes, contextual factors, and the meanings participants attach to their choices.

**Sampling**

The survey employed stratified sampling across six districts and five age bands, using quotas set against the most recent census distribution. Recruitment combined an online panel provider with an intercept sample at four transit interchanges, the latter intended to reduce digital-access bias. Wave one achieved 1,247 completed responses from 2,012 who started, a completion rate of 62 percent, with a median completion time of eleven minutes.

Interview participants were recruited from survey respondents who consented to follow-up, selected purposively across three user categories — frequent, occasional, and lapsed users — with ten in each category. **Interview procedure**

Thirty semi-structured interviews were conducted remotely, running between 35 and 55 minutes. All were recorded and transcribed with consent. A single topic guide covered journey context, decision factors, barriers, and comparison with alternatives, with probes used flexibly to allow participants to pursue their own emphases. The semi-structured format allowed consistent coverage of core topics while accommodating the divergent concerns that emerged across the three user categories.

**Analysis**

Survey data were analysed using descriptive statistics and logistic regression predicting current usage from demographic and access variables, weighted to the census distribution.

Interview transcripts were coded thematically. An initial inductive pass generated candidate codes from the data, followed by the application of a structured coding frame by two coders. Intercoder agreement was checked on a 20 percent subsample to assess consistency of application.

**Limitations**

Several limitations follow from the design.

*Sampling.* The survey's online panel component may under-represent residents with limited digital access; the intercept sample at transit interchanges partially addresses this but introduces its own bias, as it over-represents those who use transit at those locations. The 62 percent completion rate means that non-response bias is possible. The interview sample is purposive and small, and findings from it should not be read as generalisable; they are intended to identify mechanisms and divergent perspectives.

*Procedure.* The one-to-ten split across the three user categories is designed for balance but does not reflect relative prevalence in the population; frequent users are over-sampled relative to their share of the population, which is acceptable for exploratory purposes but is a deliberate skew. Remote interviews may have produced shorter or less natural responses than face-to-face interviews, though this remains unknown. All interviews relied on self-report, which is subject to recall and social desirability bias — P04 noted having "never sat down and worked out" their costs, suggesting that retrospective accounts of usage should be treated with caution.

*Analysis.* The inductive-then-structured approach means that themes are shaped by what participants emphasised, but the two-coder agreement check can inflate apparent reliability if both coders have been trained on the same frame; this is a known limitation of this approach. ## METHODS SECTION

### Design

This study used a mixed-methods design, with a repeated cross-sectional survey and a qualitative semi-structured interview component, running from January to September. The two components were designed to be complementary: the survey to estimate prevalence and associations across the population, and the interviews to understand decision-making, experience, and the reasons behind observed patterns in ways a fixed-response instrument cannot capture.

### Sampling

**Survey.** Three survey waves were fielded in February, May, and August, each targeting 1,200 completed responses. Sampling was stratified by district and age band against the most recent census distribution, with quotas for six districts and five age bands. Recruitment used an online panel provider, supplemented by an intercept sample at four transit interchanges to reduce digital-access bias. Wave one achieved 1,247 completed responses, with a 62 percent completion rate among those who started; median completion time was eleven minutes.

**Interviews.** Thirty semi-structured interviews were conducted with survey respondents who consented to follow-up. Purposive selection was used to construct three user categories of ten each: frequent users, occasional users, and lapsed users. This sampling approach was designed to ensure variation on the dimension most directly relevant to the research questions (current relationship to the service) while allowing within-category diversity in other characteristics. Interviews ran between 35 and 55 minutes, conducted remotely, recorded and transcribed with consent.

### Procedure

The survey instrument captured demographics, access to transport options, usage patterns of micro-mobility services, and attitudes toward alternatives. The interview topic guide covered journey context, decision factors, barriers, and comparisons with alternatives, with probes used flexibly to follow participant-led lines of discussion. Interviews were semi-structured: the guide ensured comparability across participants, but flexibility in probing allowed participants to raise considerations — such as infrastructure quality, safety perceptions, and self-identity relative to other users, which all emerged in the interviews — that the research team had not anticipated in full.

### Analysis

Survey data were analysed with descriptive statistics and logistic regression predicting current usage from demographic and access variables, weighted to the census distribution. Interview transcripts were coded thematically. The approach involved an initial inductive pass to identify emergent themes, followed by the application of a structured coding frame by two coders. Intercoder agreement was checked on a 20 percent subsample. The analysis was iterative: themes identified in the first coding pass were refined and, where necessary, split or merged in consultation between coders, with the final frame applied consistently across all thirty transcripts.

### Ethics

The study was approved by the institutional review committee. Participants in both components consented to participation; interview participants additionally consented to recording for transcription and to the use of anonymised quotations. A prize draw entry was offered as an incentive for the survey; no other incentives were used.

Analysis of the three semi-structured interviews reveals three overarching themes, each with internal variation and points of disagreement between participants. ## METHODS

### Design

The study used a mixed-methods design running from January to September. Two components were pursued in parallel: a repeated cross-sectional survey of city residents, and semi-structured interviews with a purposive subsample of survey respondents. The survey provided breadth and trend data across three waves; the interviews provided depth on decision processes, barriers, and comparisons with alternatives.

### Sampling

Survey sampling was stratified by district and age band against the most recent census distribution, with quotas set for six districts and five age bands. Recruitment used an online panel provider, supplemented by an intercept sample at four transit interchanges to reduce digital-access bias. This introduces both a strength and a limitation: panel recruitment risks self-selection among the digitally comfortable, while intercept recruitment captures a different set of biases (transit users specifically, at specific locations, during specific times). The supplementation mitigates but does not eliminate coverage error.

Interview participants were recruited from survey respondents who consented to follow-up, with purposive selection across three user categories: frequent, occasional, and lapsed users, ten in each. This design ensures variation on the central behavioural variable but inherits the survey's sampling frame limitations — the interview subsample cannot include people who never completed the survey.

### Interview procedure

Thirty interviews were conducted remotely, each running between 35 and 55 minutes. All were recorded and transcribed with consent. A single topic guide covered journey context, decision factors, barriers, and comparisons with alternatives, with probes used flexibly.

Remote interviewing enabled participation across districts but introduced limitations: participants without reliable connectivity or comfort with remote formats may be underrepresented in consent and completion. The 35–55 minute range also suggests variability in depth; transcripts should be checked for whether shorter interviews covered the full topic guide.

### Analysis

Survey data were analysed with descriptive statistics and logistic regression predicting current usage from demographic and access variables, weighted to the census distribution. Weighting corrects for known demographic skew but cannot correct for unmeasured differences between respondents and non-respondents — a standard limitation of weighted survey analysis.

Interview transcripts were coded thematically, with an initial inductive pass followed by a structured coding frame applied by two coders. Intercoder agreement was checked on a 20 percent subsample. This two-stage approach balances openness with consistency, but the single-topic-guide design across a three-category sample means within-category variation may be under-analysed; exploring themes by user category would strengthen the account.

### Limitations

The mixed-methods design does not support causal claims. There is no baseline measurement of attitudes or behaviour before any specific intervention, and no control group. The survey describes associations only; the interviews provide accounts of reasoning that participants themselves construct retrospectively.

The three interviews analysed here are a partial subset of the thirty conducted. Any reading of "findings" from these three must be treated as illustrative rather than representative, and should be checked against the full corpus before generalising.

# Thematic Findings Section

## Overview

Across the three interviews, participants described a complex relationship with micromobility services that shifted between convenience, cost, infrastructure, and identity. While each participant occupied a different position on the usage spectrum — daily commuter, occasional weekend user, and former regular user — several themes recurred, and important points of tension emerged around infrastructure, cost, and the social dimensions of use.

## Theme 1: Time and predictability as primary drivers

The most consistent theme across all three interviews was time: specifically, the value of predictable journey times and the role micromobility plays in reducing the uncertainty associated with public transport.

Participant 04, a daily commuter, framed this most directly:

> "I started using them because the bus was unreliable in the mornings, not because I wanted to. The stop is a ten minute walk and then whatever comes, comes. With a scooter I know roughly what the journey takes. It's about fifteen minutes door to door and it varies by two or three either way."

For Participant 04, the appeal was not speed per se, but the *variance reduction* — a journey that was predictable within a narrow range replaced one that could vary widely depending on bus frequency.

Participant 11, the occasional user, similarly framed time as the central consideration, though for a more specific trip purpose:

> "I use them for one specific thing, which is getting to the sports centre on Saturday mornings. It's an awkward journey otherwise. Two buses or a twenty-five minute walk with a bag."

Here, the comparison was against both public transport and walking, and the service won on the specific geometry of a single recurring journey.

Participant 19's account of why they stopped is revealing precisely because time also structured the exit decision:

> "After that I started taking longer routes and the time advantage disappeared, and once the time advantage disappeared the cost stopped making sense."

This suggests that time savings function as the *enabling condition* for cost acceptability. When the time advantage eroded, cost became salient. All three participants, then, organised their accounts around time, but the way time interacted with other factors differed.

## Theme 2: Infrastructure and safety as the binding constraint

The clearest point of divergence — and also the strongest convergence — concerned infrastructure. Participant 19, the former user, was unequivocal about what would bring them back:

> "I'd go back if the lanes were there. That's the honest answer. Not cheaper vehicles, not more vehicles. The lanes. There's a stretch of maybe eight hundred metres near the ring road that's the problem, and everyone I know who rides has a view about that stretch."

The near-miss incident was the precipitating event, but the infrastructure itself was the underlying condition:

> "I'd been riding on roads I now think were not safe, and I'd been doing it because the alternative routes took longer."

Crucially, Participant 19's continued use of the same vehicles in other cities — "same vehicles, same app, completely different experience because of what's around them" — locates the problem firmly in the built environment rather than in the service itself.

Participant 11, who uses bikes rather than scooters, described a *different* kind of infrastructure problem: parking provision, which affected the practical convenience of use.

> "You're meant to leave them in the bays and the bay near the sports centre is often full, so you're circling around looking for somewhere legitimate, which adds five minutes to a fifteen minute journey. I've been fined once for leaving one outside a bay and I've been careful since."

Whereas Participant 19's concern was about safety infrastructure (protected lanes), Participant 11's was about the physical infrastructure of parking and how the service's rules interacted with its availability. Both are infrastructure concerns, but they operate at different scales and produce different failure modes: one a hard safety threshold that terminates use, the other a cumulative inconvenience that erodes goodwill.

Participant 04 did not mention infrastructure directly, which is itself noteworthy — as a daily user whose journeys are working reliably, infrastructure was invisible. It became salient only when it failed:

> "When there isn't [a scooter available within a couple of streets], it throws the whole morning, and that happens maybe once a fortnight. That's the thing that would push me back to the bus if it got worse."

## Theme 3: Cost as an opaque but potentially decisive factor

All three participants referenced cost, but with very different degrees of engagement. Participant 04, the most frequent user, was strikingly disengaged:

> "Cost is the part I've stopped thinking about, which is probably bad. It's about four pounds each way and I've never sat down and worked out what that comes to. My partner did and was not impressed."

This suggests that for the daily user, cost had been absorbed into a routine where the perceived time benefits dominated. The active avoidance — "I've never sat down and worked out what that comes to" — is interesting. It reads as a deliberate non-calculation, possibly because the arithmetic would be uncomfortable.

Participant 11 raised cost less as a personal barrier than as a structural one, connected to the service's apparent target demographic:

Participant 19 described cost as acceptable *only when* the time advantage held:

> "Once the time advantage disappeared the cost stopped making sense."

## Theme 4: Weather and seasonality as an overlooked structuring factor

Participant 04 was the only interviewee to raise weather, but their account suggests it is a significant determinant of actual use patterns:

> "I don't ride in the rain. If it's wet I walk to the station and take the train, which takes longer but I'm not arriving soaked. So my usage is very seasonal, much more than I would have guessed before I looked at my own trip history."

This has implications for how usage data should be interpreted and for how services are planned. Neither Participant 11 nor Participant 19 raised weather, which may reflect their lower overall usage frequency, or the specific journeys in question (weekend sports trips, holiday riding in cities with better infrastructure). But Participant 04's account suggests weather is a primary driver of modal substitution: when they cannot ride, they do not switch to the bus on the same route; they take a *different* mode (train) that protects them from arriving wet.

The three interviews diverge on several points:

**What failure looks like.** For Participant 04, failure is infrequent (once a fortnight) and recoverable, but cumulative. The design comprised two complementary components: a repeated cross-sectional survey of city residents, and semi-structured interviews with a purposive subsample of survey respondents. The survey was designed to provide population-level estimates of usage and to identify correlates of use; the interviews were designed to provide depth and explanation around decision-making processes, barriers, and the lived experience of adopting, using, or abandoning micromobility. The mixed-methods approach was chosen to allow triangulation between statistical patterns and interpretive accounts.

**Sampling and survey**

The survey was administered in three waves (February, May, August), each targeting 1,200 completed responses. Sampling was stratified by district and age band against the most recent census distribution, with quotas set for the six districts and five age bands. Recruitment used an online panel provider supplemented by an intercept sample at four transit interchanges, the latter intended to reduce digital-access bias. Wave one achieved 1,247 completed responses, with a 62 percent completion rate among those who started. Median completion time was eleven minutes.

**Interview sampling and procedure**

Thirty semi-structured interviews were conducted with survey respondents who had consented to follow-up. Interviewees were purposively selected to yield three user categories—frequent users, occasional users, and lapsed users—with ten in each. This purposive approach was intended to capture the full range of engagement with micromobility, including those who had rejected it. Interviews lasted between 35 and 55 minutes and were conducted remotely. All were recorded and transcribed with participants' consent. A single topic guide was used across all interviews, covering journey context, decision factors, barriers, and comparison with alternatives; interviewers used probes flexibly to pursue emergent lines of inquiry.

**Analysis**

Survey data were analysed descriptively and with logistic regression predicting current usage from demographic and access variables, with responses weighted to the census distribution. This allowed identification of the demographic and spatial correlates of usage at each wave and across waves. Interview transcripts were analysed thematically. An initial inductive pass generated broad themes; a structured coding frame was then applied by two coders to the full corpus, with intercoder agreement checked on a 20 percent subsample to ensure reliability of the coding scheme. The three interviews quoted in this report are drawn from this corpus and are illustrative of the wider thematic patterns.

**Ethics**

The study was approved by the institutional review committee. All participants gave informed consent to recording and to anonymised quotation. No financial incentives were offered beyond entry into a prize draw, which was designed to encourage participation without unduly influencing responses.

**Limitations**

The design carries several limitations, each traceable to a specific methodological choice.

*Limitations from the survey.* The reliance on an online panel, even with an intercept supplement, means the sample may still under-represent residents with limited internet access or low digital confidence. The 62 percent completion rate in wave one also raises the possibility of non-response bias: those who completed may differ systematically from those who did not on relevant attitudinal or behavioural dimensions. The repeated cross-sectional design tracks population-level change but does not follow individuals over time; consequently, transitions between user categories (e.g., frequent to lapsed) cannot be observed directly in the survey data.

**Working notes, micromobility adoption study**

--- Session, week 3 ---

Kickoff with the transport authority team. Four people on their side: programme lead, data analyst, two policy officers. Programme lead is the decision maker, the analyst is the one who will actually use anything we produce.

Their stated question is whether to renew the operator permits on current terms. Underneath that, what they actually want to know is whether micromobility is pulling people off buses or out of cars, because the answer changes the political case entirely. Nobody said this outright. It came out when the policy officers started talking about the bus contract renewal in the same breath.

Decided: we ask about substituted mode explicitly in the survey rather than inferring it from trip data. Inference from trip data was the original plan and it would not have answered their real question.

Open question: whether we can get operator trip data at all. The analyst thinks two of the six will share, the others will refuse on commercial grounds. We should not design anything that depends on having it.

Action taken: drafted the substituted-mode question with a forced choice and an other-with-text option. Tested it on five colleagues, three of them picked "other" and wrote something that mapped to an existing category, so the categories need better labels.

--- Session, week 5 ---

Reworked the substituted-mode question. Categories now: walked, bus, train or tram, drove myself, got a lift, taxi or ride-hailing, would not have made the trip. Added the last one after a colleague pointed out that induced demand is a real category and we would otherwise be forcing people to name a mode they never used.

Sampling discussion. Original plan was a straight online panel. Problem is that the population most likely to be excluded from micromobility is also the population least likely to be on an online panel, so we would systematically miss the people the equity part of the brief cares about.

Decided: add an intercept sample at transit interchanges. Four sites, chosen to span the district income distribution. This costs about three weeks of fieldwork time and we have absorbed it by cutting the planned pilot from two waves to one.

Tried and abandoned: a household mail-out to boost coverage in the two outer districts. Costed it and it came to more than the entire interview budget for a projected 200 responses. Not proceeding.

Open question: whether the intercept sample can be weighted together with the panel sample without producing something indefensible. The analyst on their side has views. We should ask before wave one rather than after.

--- Session, week 6 ---

Weighting question resolved, provisionally. We treat them as one sample with a source indicator, weight to the census distribution on district and age, and report source as a covariate in the models. If source turns out to predict the outcome after weighting, we report that rather than hiding it.

Ethics submission went in. Two questions came back: whether we are recording interviews with under-18s, which we are not, and whether the prize draw constitutes an inducement. Answered both, expecting approval next week.

Topic guide drafted for the interviews. Four sections: how they travel now, what prompted them to try micromobility or not, what would change their behaviour, and how it compares to what they used before. Deliberately did not include a section on cost, because cost comes up unprompted in every pilot conversation and asking about it directly seems to produce a rehearsed answer.

Noted for later: the pilot conversations all produced a weather answer within the first two minutes. If that holds in the real interviews, seasonality is a bigger part of this story than the brief assumes, and the authority's own usage figures are annual averages that would hide it entirely.
