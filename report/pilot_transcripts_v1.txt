**Executive Summary: Q3 Micromobility Adoption Study**

Fieldwork is complete, comprising three survey waves and thirty interviews. This report consolidates the evidence to inform the permit renewal decision for shared micromobility services.

**Key Findings**

**1. Usage Exceeds Expectations and Operator Records**
Between 29% and 33% of residents report using shared micromobility in the past year (wave three: 31.4%, CI 28.8–34.0, n=1,208, census-weighted). These figures exceed operator-reported user counts. Two explanations fit the data: recall error inflating self-reports, or operator undercounting due to shared household accounts. Current evidence cannot distinguish between them.

**2. The Substitution Pattern Weakens the Environmental Case**
When asked what mode they would otherwise have used, respondents named:
- Walking: 34.1%
- Bus: 26.8%
- Train/tram: 14.2%
- Private car: 11.3%
- Would not have travelled: 8.9%

This pattern is stable across all waves and districts (controlling for income). A logistic model predicting car substitution explains minimal variance (pseudo R² = 0.08), indicating car substitution is not concentrated in any identifiable demographic.

**Implication:** Micromobility is primarily replacing walking and bus journeys, not car trips. The environmental rationale for renewal is therefore weaker than currently framed; the access rationale is stronger.

**3. Barriers Are Infrastructure-Based, Not Vehicle-Based**
Lapsed users cited route safety and lack of protected lanes more often than cost, availability, or vehicle quality. One ring-road stretch was named unprompted by multiple participants. Among non-users, safety ranks second only to cost as a barrier.

**4. Three Limitations Require Acknowledgment**
- Seasonal variation is partially confounded with change over time (waves one and two fell in February and May); a stable annual-usage question was added in wave two.
- Four of six operators refused trip-data access; all usage figures rely on self-report.
- District data required postcode recoding for ~9% of wave one responses (documented in methods).

**5. An Emergent Theme Beyond the Brief**
Several interviewees described a shift from timetable-checking to spontaneous journey planning. If generalizable, this indicates a value in micromobility not captured by trip counts—and it may inform how service reliability is specified in permit conditions.

**Recommendation**

**Renew on revised terms, not current terms.** Three changes are indicated:

1. **Protected-lane provision:** Permit conditions tied to protected-lane delivery on routes carrying the highest trip volumes.
2. **Parking bay requirements:** Modeled on the Lyon and Gothenburg schemes.
3. **Reporting condition:** A requirement to resolve the discrepancy between operator counts and self-reported usage.

**Executive Summary: Q3 Micromobility Adoption Study**
**To:** Permit Review Committee
**From:** Research Consultancy
**Date:** Q3 2024
**Subject:** Key Findings and Recommendations for the Permit Renewal Decision

**Overview**
Fieldwork for the micromobility adoption study is complete, encompassing three survey waves and thirty in-depth interviews. This summary presents the key findings relevant to the upcoming permit renewal decision, focusing on usage rates, modal substitution, barriers to use, and study limitations.

**1. Usage is Higher than Reported**
Self-reported usage is robust and exceeds operator-provided figures. Across all waves, 29–33% of residents report using shared micromobility in the past year (Wave 3: 31.4%, 95% CI 28.8–34.0). This discrepancy likely stems from recall error in surveys or operator undercounting due to shared household accounts, but the available data cannot distinguish between these explanations.

**2. The Evidence Weakens the Environmental Case but Strengthens the Equity Case**
The most critical finding for the renewal decision concerns what trips micromobility replaces. Users most frequently substitute walking (34.1%) and bus journeys (26.8%), with only 11.3% replacing private car trips. This pattern is stable across all waves and demographic groups. The low explanatory power of our model for car substitution (pseudo R²=0.08) indicates that car replacement is not concentrated in any identifiable segment. Consequently, the current framing that micromobility primarily reduces car usage is unsupported. Instead, its primary benefit is expanding mobility access, particularly for pedestrians and bus riders.

**3. Usage Barriers are Infrastructure-Based**
The primary deterrent to usage is infrastructure quality, not vehicle or cost issues. Both lapsed users and non-users consistently identified route safety and the lack of protected lanes as decisive factors. Interview participants specifically highlighted one road near the ring road as a significant obstacle. This suggests that targeted infrastructure investment could yield significant ridership gains.

**4. Three Limitations to Weigh**
The findings should be considered with three caveats:
- **Seasonality:** Waves 1 and 2 were conducted in February and May, potentially confounding seasonal variation with changes over time.
- **Data Reliance:** All usage figures are self-reported, as four of six operators refused to share trip data.
- **Data Quality:** Approximately 9% of wave-one district data required recoding due to an instrument problem, a limitation documented in the full methods.

**5. An Emerging Theme: A Shift in Mobility Behavior**
Interviews revealed a novel theme outside the original brief: users are shifting from schedule-based planning to spontaneous departure. If this trend generalizes, it represents a value not captured by traditional trip-count metrics and suggests that permit conditions should specify service reliability standards to support this emergent behavior.

**Recommendation**
The evidence supports **renewal on revised terms, rather than renewal on current terms**. We recommend the following three conditions be attached to the permit:

1.  **Infrastructure Linkage:** Tie permit conditions to the provision of protected lanes on the specific routes carrying the highest trip volumes.
2.  **Parking Standards:** Adopt a parking bay requirement modeled on successful schemes in Lyon and Gothenburg to manage sidewalk clutter and access.
3.  **Reporting Requirement:** Mandate a standardized reporting condition to resolve the discrepancy between operator counts and self-reported usage, enabling accurate future performance evaluation.

## Methods Section

**Design.** This study employed a mixed-methods design running from January to September, combining a repeated cross-sectional survey of city residents with semi-structured interviews. The survey component allowed for tracking of usage patterns and attitudes over time, while the interviews provided depth on decision-making processes and lived experience that survey items could not capture. The mixed-methods structure was intended to balance breadth and depth: the survey provides a representative picture across six districts and five age bands, while the interviews allow the research team to interrogate the "why" behind observed patterns.

**Sampling.** Survey sampling was stratified by district and age band against the most recent census distribution, with quotas set across six districts and five age bands. Recruitment ran through an online panel provider supplemented by an intercept sample at four transit interchanges to reduce digital-access bias, recognising that exclusive reliance on online panels risks excluding residents who are less digitally connected, who are also likely to be underrepresented among micromobility users. This dual approach was selected to mitigate that bias. Wave one (February) achieved 1,247 completed responses, with a 62 percent completion rate among those who began the survey, against a target of 1,200.

Interview participants were recruited from the survey sample; respondents who consented to follow-up contact were purposively selected to fill three user categories: frequent users, occasional users, and lapsed users, with ten participants in each category. This purposive structure was designed to ensure sufficient representation of lapsed users, a group often underrepresented in studies of active transport modes because they are no longer captured by prevalence-based recruitment. The three-way split allowed for within-category patterns to emerge while still permitting comparison across the adoption lifecycle.

One limitation follows from this approach: the interview sample is necessarily drawn from survey respondents rather than from the general population, meaning the sample may over-represent people with stronger opinions about micromobility (those willing to consent to follow-up). With ten participants in each category, the sample is not intended to be statistically generalisable; rather, it was selected for analytic depth.

**Interview procedure.** Thirty semi-structured interviews ran between 35 and 55 minutes, conducted remotely, and recorded and transcribed with consent. A single topic guide covered four areas: journey context, decision factors, barriers, and comparison with alternatives, with probes used flexibly to explore emergent themes. Remote delivery was chosen for participant convenience and consistency of recording quality. A limitation of the remote format is that non-verbal cues are less accessible, and screen-based interaction can be less natural than in-person conversation, potentially reducing rapport and candour on sensitive topics (although little sensitive material was expected given the subject matter). The topic guide was used flexibly in line with good semi-structured practice; this means that not all themes were covered with equal depth across all interviews, which should be borne in mind when interpreting the thematic findings below.

**Analysis.** Survey data were analysed using descriptive statistics and logistic regression predicting current usage from demographic and access variables, weighted to the census distribution to correct for known biases in the achieved sample. One limitation follows from the weighting approach: weighting corrects for demographic representativeness but cannot adjust for unmeasured differences between responders and non-responders, such as attitudinal or interest-based differences.

Interview transcripts were coded thematically, with an initial inductive pass to surface emergent themes followed by a structured coding frame applied by two coders. Intercoder agreement was checked on a 20 percent subsample. This two-stage approach was chosen to balance discovery with consistency: the inductive pass allowed themes to arise from the data rather than being imposed by the research team's prior assumptions, while the structured frame ensured systematic comparison across transcripts. # Working notes, micromobility adoption study

--- Session, week 3 ---

Kickoff with the transport authority team. Four people on their side: programme lead, data analyst, two policy officers. Programme lead is the decision maker, the analyst is the one who will actually use anything we produce.

Their stated question is whether to renew the operator permits on current terms. Underneath that, what they actually want to know is whether micromobility is pulling people off buses or out of cars, because the answer changes the political case entirely. Nobody said this outright. It came out when the policy officers started talking about the bus contract renewal in the same breath.

Decided: we ask about substituted mode explicitly in the survey rather than inferring it from trip data. Inference from trip data was the original plan and it would not have answered their real question.

Open question: whether we can get operator trip data at all. The analyst thinks two of the six will share, the others will refuse on commercial grounds. We should not design anything that depends on having it.

Action taken: drafted the substituted-mode question with a forced choice and an other-with-text option. Tested it on five colleagues, three of them picked "other" and wrote something that mapped to an existing category, so the categories need better labels.

--- Session, week 5 ---

Reworked the substituted-mode question. Categories now: walked, bus, train or tram, drove myself, got a lift, taxi or ride-hailing, would not have made the trip. Added the last one after a colleague pointed out that induced demand is a real category and we would otherwise be forcing people to name a mode they never used.

Sampling discussion. Original plan was a straight online panel. Problem is that the population most likely to be excluded from micromobility is also the population least likely to be on an online panel, so we would systematically miss the people the equity part of the brief cares about.

Decided: add an intercept sample at transit interchanges. Four sites, chosen to span the district income distribution. This costs about three weeks of fieldwork time and we have absorbed it by cutting the planned pilot from two waves to one.

Tried and abandoned: a household mail-out to boost coverage in the two outer districts. Costed it and it came to more than the entire interview budget for a projected 200 responses. Not proceeding.

Open question: whether the intercept sample can be weighted together with the panel sample without producing something indefensible. The analyst on their side has views. We should ask before wave one rather than after.

--- Session, week 6 ---

Weighting question resolved, provisionally. We treat them as one sample with a source indicator, weight to the census distribution on district and age, and report source as a covariate in the models. If source turns out to predict the outcome after weighting, we report that rather than hiding it.

Ethics submission went in. Two questions came back: whether we are recording interviews with under-18s, which we are not, and whether the prize draw constitutes an inducement. Answered both, expecting approval next week.

Topic guide drafted for the interviews. Four sections: how they travel now, what prompted them to try micromobility or not, what would change their behaviour, and how it compares to what they used before. Deliberately did not include a section on cost, because cost comes up unprompted in every pilot conversation and asking about it directly seems to produce a rehearsed answer.

Noted for later: the pilot conversations all produced a weather answer within the first two minutes. If that holds in the real interviews, seasonality is a bigger part of this story than the brief assumes, and the authority's own usage figures are annual averages that would hide it entirely.

--- Session, week 8 ---

Wave one fieldwork opened. 1,247 completed responses against a target of 1,200. Completion rate 62

# Working notes, micromobility adoption study

--- Session, week 3 ---

Kickoff with the transport authority team. Four people on their side: programme lead, data analyst, two policy officers. Programme lead is the decision maker, the analyst is the one who will actually use anything we produce.

Their stated question is whether to renew the operator permits on current terms. Underneath that, what they actually want to know is whether micromobility is pulling people off buses or out of cars, because the answer changes the political case entirely. Nobody said this outright. It came out when the policy officers started talking about the bus contract renewal in the same breath.

Decided: we ask about substituted mode explicitly in the survey rather than inferring it from trip data. Inference from trip data was the original plan and it would not have answered their real question.

Open question: whether we can get operator trip data at all. The analyst thinks two of the six will share, the others will refuse on commercial grounds. We should not design anything that depends on having it.

Action taken: drafted the substituted-mode question with a forced choice and an other-with-text option. Tested it on five colleagues, three of them picked "other" and wrote something that mapped to an existing category, so the categories need better labels.

--- Session, week 5 ---

Reworked the substituted-mode question. Categories now: walked, bus, train or tram, drove myself, got a lift, taxi or ride-hailing, would not have made the trip. Added the last one after a colleague pointed out that induced demand is a real category and we would otherwise be forcing people to name a mode they never used.

Sampling discussion. Original plan was a straight online panel. Problem is that the population most likely to be excluded from micromobility is also the population least likely to be on an online panel, so we would systematically miss the people the equity part of the brief cares about.

Decided: add an intercept sample at transit interchanges. Four sites, chosen to span the district income distribution. This costs about three weeks of fieldwork time and we have absorbed it by cutting the planned pilot from two waves to one.

Tried and abandoned: a household mail-out to boost coverage in the two outer districts. Costed it and it came to more than the entire interview budget for a projected 200 responses. Not proceeding.

Open question: whether the intercept sample can be weighted together with the panel sample without producing something indefensible. The analyst on their side has views. We should ask before wave one rather than after.

--- Session, week 6 ---

Weighting question resolved, provisionally. We treat them as one sample with a source indicator, weight to the census distribution on district and age, and report source as a covariate in the models. If source turns out to predict the outcome after weighting, we report that rather than hiding it.

Ethics submission went in. Two questions came back: whether we are recording interviews with under-18s, which we are not, and whether the prize draw constitutes an inducement. Answered both, expecting approval next week.

Topic guide drafted for the interviews. Four sections: how they travel now, what prompted them to try micromobility or not, what would change their behaviour, and how it compares to what they used before. Deliberately did not include a section on cost, because cost comes up unprompted in every pilot conversation and asking about it directly seems to produce a rehearsed answer.

Noted for later: the pilot conversations all produced a weather answer within the first two minutes. If that holds in the real interviews, seasonality is a bigger part of this story than the brief assumes, and the authority's own usage figures are annual averages that would hide it entirely.

--- Session, week 8 ---

Wave one fieldwork opened. 1,247 completed responses against a target of 1,200. Completion rate 62
