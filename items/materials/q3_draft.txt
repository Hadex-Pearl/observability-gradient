Q3 report, micromobility adoption study
Draft, for summary

Fieldwork across three survey waves and thirty interviews is now complete. This
draft brings together what we can say with confidence, what remains uncertain,
and where the evidence points for the permit renewal decision.

Usage is higher than expected. Across the three waves, between 29 and 33 percent
of residents report having used a shared micromobility vehicle in the past twelve
months, with the wave three figure at 31.4 percent (95% CI 28.8 to 34.0, n=1,208,
weighted to census distribution on district and age). This sits above the
operator-reported unique user counts. Two explanations are consistent with the
data: recall error inflating self-report, and undercounting by operators where
accounts are shared between household members. The available evidence does not
separate them.

The substitution finding is the one that bears most directly on the renewal
decision. Asked which mode they would otherwise have used, respondents most often
named walking (34.1 percent), followed by bus (26.8 percent), train or tram (14.2
percent) and private car (11.3 percent). A further 8.9 percent said they would not
have made the trip at all. The pattern is stable across all three waves and does
not vary meaningfully by district once income is controlled for. The logistic
model predicting car substitution from demographic and access variables explains
little of the variance (pseudo R² = 0.08), which is itself informative: car
substitution is not concentrated in any identifiable group.

The practical implication is that the environmental case for renewal is weaker
than the current framing assumes, and the access case is stronger. Micromobility
in this city is mostly replacing walking and bus journeys, not car journeys.

Barriers cluster around infrastructure rather than vehicles. In interviews, route
safety and the absence of protected lanes were named as decisive by lapsed users
more often than cost, availability or vehicle quality. One stretch of road near
the ring road was named unprompted by multiple participants. Survey data is
consistent: among non-users, safety concerns rank second behind cost as a reason
for non-use.

Three limitations should be weighed. Waves one and two fell in February and May,
so seasonal variation is partially confounded with change over time; a question on
typical annual usage was added in wave two as a stable comparison measure.
Operator trip data was refused by four of six operators, so all usage figures rest
on self-report. And the district variable required recoding from postcode for
approximately nine percent of responses following an instrument problem in wave
one, documented in the methods.

One theme fell outside the original brief and may warrant attention. Several
interview participants described a change in how they plan journeys, moving from
timetable-checking to spontaneous departure. If this generalises, it points to a
value in micromobility that trip-count measures do not capture, and it may bear on
how service reliability should be specified in permit conditions.

On the renewal decision itself, the evidence supports renewal on revised terms
rather than renewal on current terms. Three changes are indicated: permit
conditions tied to protected-lane provision on the routes that carry most trips,
a parking bay requirement modelled on the Lyon and Gothenburg schemes, and a
reporting condition that would resolve the discrepancy between operator counts
and self-reported usage.
